    UchQadam

    I launched a hyperlocal grocery delivery service, beat the only alternative 3× on speed and 3× on price, and got zero orders. This is what actually broke.

    Duration
    14 days Jul 2026 — launched & killed
    Role
    Solo — everything
    Market
    Samarkand, Uzbekistan

    A real MVP with real distribution. Shut down against kill criteria set before launch.

    Overview

    People in my neighborhood were already paying a local guy about 5,000 so'm to fetch things from the shop. Nobody had marketed that to them — it was just happening. That's the strongest kind of demand evidence: an existing transaction, not a stated intention.

    My hypothesis was simple. Formalize what already happens informally, and the behavior transfers.

    It didn't. And the reason turned out to be more useful than the idea.

    0-to-1 Concierge MVP Funnel Analysis Growth Experiment Kill Decision

    The Challenge

    How might I turn an informal, trust-based neighborhood errand into a service people will pay a premium for?

    The context: One neighborhood — roughly 500 apartments, 1,500–2,000 people, a 500-metre radius. No hyperlocal delivery existed. The only alternative was Korzinka, a supermarket chain: 45–60 minutes, 15,000 so'm delivery fee.

    My offer: ~15 minutes, 4,900 so'm. Three times faster, three times cheaper. I charged deliberately rather than going free — I wanted to know whether people would pay for convenience, not whether they'd accept something free.

    What Happened

    Doors reached
    250
    QR scan rate
    12%
    Conversations started
    2
    Orders completed
    0

    250 flyers → ~30 scans → 2 conversations → 0 orders. Killed 14 days after launch, against criteria set before it.

    A 12% scan rate on cold physical distribution is strong — direct-mail benchmarks run 1–5%. Attention was never the problem. Two things broke after it, at two different steps, and they needed different fixes.

    What I Built

    A concierge MVP, deliberately minimal — no app, no custom software.

    Flyer with QR code → Telegram bot → shop's staff group chat → replies relayed back to the customer. Menu buttons handled order status once an order was placed. One employee from the partner shop handled delivery. Total build: about five hours and effectively zero cash — flyers printed at home.

    📄 The marketing

    Bilingual Uzbek/Russian flyer. The front led with speed and price. The back ran six concrete use cases rather than generic copy:

    • "It's +40° outside and there's no ice cream?"
    • "Bread's finished and guests are on the way?"
    • "It's 9pm and your child needs milk?"
    • "Sunday morning and there's no bread?"

    This part worked. It's why 12% of doors scanned a QR code from a service they'd never heard of.

    The Diagnosis

    1. My flyer was better than my product

    The flyer built specific intent — six named situations where you'd want this. Then people scanned, hit Start, got a greeting, and faced a blank text prompt. No catalog. No prices. No list of what the shop stocked.

    To order, you had to already know what you wanted, know the shop carried it, and type it out. 28 of 30 people never typed a word.

    I designed the entire conversation flow and never asked what a user sees in the first ten seconds. Carrying the flyer's own use cases and a categorized price list into the bot's first message would have taken an hour.

    2. The service failed on both people who engaged

    Two people got past the blank prompt and messaged. The shop didn't reply in time. Both were lost. That's a 100% failure rate on the only customers I ever had.

    The partner had no stake — no money in, no real effort, and indifferent when I shut it down. I'd handed the single most important step in my own experiment to someone with nothing to lose, and I had no leverage when they went quiet.

    3. The failures compound — which inverts the fix order

    This is the part I only saw afterward. Fixing the catalog first would have made things worse.

    Suppose a catalog lifted scan-to-message from 7% to 40%. That's roughly 12 conversations instead of 2 — all of them hitting the same unresponsive shop. In a 500-apartment neighborhood where people talk, I'd have converted a quiet failure into a public one and burned the only market I had.

    Acquisition fixes are actively harmful when the service underneath is broken.

    4. What I still don't know

    I beat the incumbent 3× on speed and 3× on price and it wasn't enough to get 28 people to type a sentence. The informal version worked because it ran on a known person — a neighbor people recognized. Mine ran on a QR code and a chatbot. I kept the logistics and removed the trust.

    The shop is also a 500-metre walk. Convenience earns a premium when the alternative genuinely costs you something, and here it barely did.

    Both are plausible. Neither is proven — because I never asked anyone.

    The Honest Conclusion

    I never tested my own hypothesis. I wanted to know whether people would pay for convenience, and I got zero transactions. That question is still open.

    This wasn't a negative result. It was no result. I built a machine that never produced a single data point about the thing I actually cared about, and I'd rather say that plainly than dress it up as "I validated that the market doesn't exist." I validated nothing about the market. I learned a great deal about my own test design.

    Two orders' worth of signal is not a sample I'd defend. What I'd defend is the decision to stop paying for a test that couldn't answer its own question.

    What I'd Do Differently

    My first instinct was raise funds, own the operations, spend more on marketing. That's a do-more-of-everything answer, and it assumes the concept was right and only execution was lacking. Zero orders doesn't support that.

    • Fix the service before the funnel. Run every order myself for the first two weeks. Non-negotiable — everything else is worthless until someone reliably answers.
    • Interview the non-converters. Twenty doors, five minutes each, one day. I had 250 doors in a 500-metre radius and never knocked on one.
    • Put the catalog in the bot's first message. Carry the flyer's six use cases and a price list into the greeting. One hour of work.
    • Instrument from message one. Log scans, inbound messages, reply times. I diagnosed this funnel by counting by hand, after killing it.
    • Then re-run acquisition — through neighborhood group chats rather than cold QR codes, since trust moves through people.

    Steps one through four cost roughly one day and no money. My original instinct was to raise capital and skip straight to step five.

    Reflection

    Five hours and effectively zero cash to invalidate a direction I'd otherwise have spent a summer on. I set kill criteria before launching — fewer than five orders a week meant stop — and when the data hit them I stopped, rather than printing another round of flyers to prove myself right.

    The most useful thing I took from it isn't about grocery delivery. It's the sequencing lesson: you fix the service before you fix the funnel, because a working funnel into a broken service just distributes the failure faster.
