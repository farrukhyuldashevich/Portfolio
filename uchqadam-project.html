    UchQadam

    I launched a hyperlocal grocery delivery service, beat the only alternative 3× on speed and 3× on price, and got zero orders. This is what actually broke.

    Duration
    1 month launched & killed
    Role
    Solo
    Market
    Samarkand, Uzbekistan

    Printed flyers, a Telegram bot, and a partner shop. I set the kill criteria before launch and stopped when the numbers hit them.

    Overview

    People in my neighborhood were already paying a local guy about 5,000 so'm to fetch things from the shop. Nobody had marketed that to them. It was just happening on its own, and that felt like better evidence than anything a survey would have given me.

    My hypothesis was simple. Formalize what already happens informally, and the behavior transfers.

    It didn't. And the reason turned out to be more useful than the idea.

    0-to-1 Concierge MVP Funnel Analysis Growth Experiment Kill Decision

    The Challenge

    How might I turn an informal, trust-based neighborhood errand into a service people will pay a premium for?

    The context: One neighborhood, roughly 500 apartments, 1,500–2,000 people, a 500-metre radius. No hyperlocal delivery existed. The only alternative was Korzinka, a supermarket chain: 45–60 minutes, 15,000 so'm delivery fee.

    My offer: ~15 minutes, 4,900 so'm. Three times faster, three times cheaper. I charged on purpose instead of giving it away, because I wanted to see whether convenience was something people would actually pay for.

    What Happened

    Doors reached
    250
    QR scan rate
    12%
    Conversations started
    2
    Orders completed
    0

    250 flyers → about 30 scans → 2 conversations → 0 orders. I ran it for two weeks, spent another week talking to the shop owner and people in the neighborhood trying to fix it, and shut it down at day 30 against the criteria I had set before launch.

    About 12% of the doors I hit scanned the code. I looked it up afterwards and direct mail usually runs somewhere between 1 and 5%, so getting attention was not where this fell apart. Whatever went wrong happened after the scan, at two separate points, and each one needed a different fix.

    What I Built

    Flyer with a QR code → Telegram bot → the shop's staff group chat → replies relayed back to the customer. One employee from the partner shop handled delivery. About five hours to build and effectively zero cash, with flyers printed at home.

    Telegram rather than an app or a website. Everyone here already opens it several times a day, so there was nothing to install and nothing to learn.

    The flyer was bilingual Uzbek and Russian. The front led with speed and price, the back with six situations where you would want it.

    Full size: front, back, posters.

    The Diagnosis

    1. I was asking people to change how they shop

    The flyer built specific intent with six named situations where you would want this. Then people scanned, hit Start, got a greeting, and landed on a blank text prompt. No catalog, no prices, nothing showing what the shop actually carried. To order, you had to already know what you wanted and type it out.

    28 of the 30 people who scanned never typed a word.

    Looking back, I do not think the bot itself was the problem so much as what it was asking for. Ordering groceries by typing a list into a chat window is a new habit here, and new habits are expensive. Getting a neighborhood to change how it shops takes months of steady spending on educating people, and I had neither the time nor the money for that. Putting the flyer's use cases and a price list into the first message would have lowered the bar. It would not have removed it.

    2. We lost both of the people who reached out

    Two people got past the prompt and messaged us. The shop did not reply in time, and we lost both of them.

    That one comes down to how I set the thing up. I handed the customer-facing step to the partner shop and trusted it would be covered, and I built no way to see or control what happened once a message came in. The partner had nothing at stake, no money in and no real effort out, so when things went quiet there was nothing for me to push on. If I ran it again I would hold the first conversation with the customer myself until I knew the handoff worked.

    3. What I still do not know

    I beat the incumbent 3× on speed and 3× on price and it wasn't enough to get 28 people to type a sentence. The informal version worked because it ran on a known person, a neighbor people recognized. Mine ran on a QR code and a chatbot. I kept the logistics and removed the trust.

    The shop is also a 500-metre walk. Convenience earns a premium when the alternative genuinely costs you something, and here it barely did.

    Both explanations are plausible, and I would want to test each one properly before betting on either.

    The Honest Conclusion

    I never tested my own hypothesis. I wanted to know whether people would pay for convenience, and I got zero transactions. That question is still open.

    This wasn't a negative result. It was no result. I built a machine that never produced a single data point about the thing I actually cared about, and I'd rather say that plainly than dress it up as "I validated that the market doesn't exist." I validated nothing about the market. I learned a great deal about my own test design.

    Two orders' worth of signal is not a sample I'd defend. What I'd defend is the decision to stop paying for a test that couldn't answer its own question.

    What I'd Do Differently

    My first instinct was raise funds, own the operations, spend more on marketing. That's a do-more-of-everything answer, and it assumes the concept was right and only execution was lacking. Zero orders doesn't support that.

    • Fix the service before the funnel. Run every order myself for the first two weeks. Non-negotiable, because everything else is worthless until someone reliably answers.
    • Interview the non-converters in week one. Twenty doors, five minutes each, one day. I did talk to people in the neighborhood, but only once the numbers were already in.
    • Put the catalog in the bot's first message. Carry the flyer's six use cases and a price list into the greeting. One hour of work.
    • Instrument from message one. Log scans, inbound messages, reply times. I diagnosed this funnel by counting by hand, after killing it.
    • Then re-run acquisition through neighborhood group chats rather than cold QR codes, since trust moves through people.

    Steps one through four cost roughly one day and no money. My original instinct was to raise capital and skip straight to step five.

    Reflection

    Five hours and effectively zero cash to invalidate a direction I'd otherwise have spent a summer on. I set kill criteria before launching, fewer than five orders a week meant stop, and when the data hit them I stopped, rather than printing another round of flyers to prove myself right.

    The most useful thing I took from it isn't about grocery delivery. It's the sequencing lesson: you fix the service before you fix the funnel, because a working funnel into a broken service just distributes the failure faster.
